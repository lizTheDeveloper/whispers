# Of Whispers, and of Characters Who Will Not Do As They Are Told

*An appreciation of Whispers in the manner of E. Gary Gygax (1938–2008), who
never saw it, never wrote a word of this, and would have had opinions. Written
in homage, with apologies, and in the certain knowledge that he would have
argued with at least half of it.*

---

It has come to the attention of the undersigned that there now exists a
campaign milieu — "Whispers," they call it — in which the participant does
**not** control his character.

Let the reader sit with that a moment, as I was obliged to.

The novice will assume an error in the description. It is no error. One does
not declare "I draw my blade and set upon the ruffian." One *suggests* it. One
leans toward the fellow and murmurs the notion into his ear, as a conscience
might, or a poor angel, or that small voice which in my experience is ignored
by adventurers and hirelings alike. The character then does as he pleases.

He may take your counsel. He may take half of it. He may decline it outright
and — this is the portion which detained me longest — he will **tell you why**.

I confess my first response was the obvious one, and I shall not pretend
otherwise: this is an abdication. The whole architecture of the game as we
built it rests upon the player's agency over his man-at-arms. Strip that away
and what remains? A spectacle. A puppet-show in which the audience is permitted
to heckle.

I have since revised my position, though not without grumbling.

## I. On the matter of trust

Observe what the designers have done with the refusals.

The character keeps an accounting of you. Not a formal morale check — nothing
so blunt — but a drift. Advise him well and he comes to lend your voice weight.
Advise him into a pit and he learns, quite correctly, that the voice in his
head is a fool.

I have refereed tables where a player spent four sessions establishing that his
word was worth nothing and suffered no consequence for it whatever, because the
rules provided no instrument by which the *fiction* could remember. Here the
fiction remembers. The instrument exists.

That is not an abdication of agency. That is agency with a *cost schedule
attached*, which is the only sort worth having.

## II. On the referee, who is a machine

The referee in this milieu is an artifice. It narrates, it adjudicates, it
paces the scenes, and it keeps the record of what has actually transpired
rather than what the participants have chosen to remember.

I shall say the obvious thing first: a machine cannot be a good Dungeon Master
in the sense I have spent a career insisting upon, because it cannot *know the
table*. It cannot see the fellow at the end who has not spoken in an hour and
requires a door opened for him. It cannot decide, on the evidence of a single
tired face, that the dungeon is long enough.

But I note with considerable interest a decision the designers made which is
more sound than most made by living referees, myself in younger days included:

**The host must choose one chair.** He may run the table, or he may play a
character at it. He may not do both.

Every referee who has ever inserted his own beloved fighter into his own
campaign should read that sentence twice, and then go and sit quietly for a
while. I have seen more campaigns ruined by a referee's pet character than by
any dragon ever statted. The designers have made the error structurally
impossible. Would that I had thought of it in 1974.

## III. On the dice, which is where I am won over entirely

Here I set aside my objections and speak plainly.

The dice are rolled by the server. Not by the player. Not by the referee. Not
— and I wish to be exact, because this is the whole of the matter — by the
*artifice which narrates the outcome*.

I had intended to stop there and call it sound. I went looking for the loophole
first, as any referee worth the title would, and I found instead that they had
got there before me.

For the narrating machine is asked to name a difficulty, and one sees at once
where that leads: an artifice which has already beheld the roll may select a
difficulty to suit the story it would prefer to tell. That is fudging, dressed
in arithmetic. I have thrown players out of my house for less.

It cannot. The difficulty it proposes is **capped** at Legendary, so no ordinary
task may be declared impossible; and it is **floored** against the character's
own skill, so no expert may be handed a difficulty he could not fail. Between
those two walls the machine may have its opinion.

Then — and this is the part at which I laughed aloud — the house does the sum
itself. Effort is the roll plus the rank. Shifts are the effort less the
difficulty. The true outcome follows from the figures and from nothing else.
And if the narrator has reported an outcome the arithmetic does not support,
the house **overrules it** and writes a line in the log saying so, in as many
words: *the DM said success, the math says otherwise.*

Nor does it stop at the verdict. Where the machine has flattered the party with
a triumph the dice did not grant, the house splices a correction into the prose
— the victory catches, the price shows, the strain tells — so that the tale
told matches the numbers rolled rather than contradicting them in front of
everyone.

Consider what has been built here. The referee is not trusted. The referee is
*audited*, every turn, by a disinterested clerk who holds the dice and checks
his sums, and who is permitted to correct him mid-sentence when he goes soft on
the table.

I have spent an unreasonable portion of my life arguing that the integrity of a
campaign rests upon the integrity of the dice. Fudge them for kindness and you
teach the table that nothing is at stake. Fudge them for drama and you teach
them that you are the author and they are the readers.

These designers do not trust their own machine with the dice, and were right not
to. I have no notes. It is the most orthodox thing in the entire enterprise, and
it sits at the dead centre of the least orthodox thing I have encountered in
some years.

## IV. The verdict, such as it is

Is it Dungeons & Dragons? It is not, and the designers do not pretend it is.

Is it a *game*? I had thought not, on first reading. I now think yes, and of a
peculiar kind: the game is not "defeat the dungeon." The game is **persuade a
mind that is not yours, and that is under no obligation to agree with you.**

That is a harder game than mine. I am not certain it is a better one. I am
entirely certain it is not the same one, and I have small patience for the
notion that everything must be.

The participant who arrives demanding to move his figure where he pleases will
depart disappointed, and I would not trouble to talk him out of it — the market
is broad and there is a dungeon for him elsewhere.

But the participant who has ever refereed a character who felt, for one
startling moment, as though he had opinions of his own?

That one should pull up a chair, and lean in, and start whispering.
